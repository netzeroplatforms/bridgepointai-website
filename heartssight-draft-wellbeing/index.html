The gap

Health checks are built around the building, not the person.

Every established provider works the same way. The person travels to a centre, gives blood, goes home, and waits for a result to arrive days later, usually by post or portal. It works. It is also the single biggest reason most people never get checked at all.

Take the assessment to where people already are and the participation problem disappears. That is the whole idea, and it is only possible if you remove the two things that tie a health check to a building: the laboratory, and the clinician.

The assessment

What actually happens in the forty minutes.

A single seated session. Nine instruments, one operator, one integrated result. The person is measured, not sampled, and the reading is complete before they stand up.

Cardiovascular

Four-limb blood pressure, heart rhythm and heart rate variability, digital pulse wave analysis for arterial elasticity.

Autonomic and stress

Heart rate variability seated and standing, orthostatic response, tension index. The measured physiology of the stress response.

Body composition

Bio-impedance for fat and lean mass, fluid distribution, phase angle and cellular integrity, waist and hip ratio.

Metabolic

Capillary blood glucose, urine chemistry, hydration and specific gravity, saliva pH.

Respiratory

Spirometry and blood oxygen saturation.

Thermal and inflammatory

Multi-site infrared surface temperature, recorded left and right, so symmetry can be compared.

Nine instruments, named.

Every one is an established, commercially available device, used within its own manufacturer’s intended purpose. Nothing here is proprietary hardware and nothing is a black box you are asked to take on trust.

01

Urine and saliva

A first morning, mid-stream urine sample and a saliva sample, both given before eating, drinking or cleaning teeth. Hydration, pH, specific gravity and glucose. The two pH readings are read as a pair rather than separately.

02

Bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy

Two contacts, at the right hand and right foot, with height, weight, waist and hip entered alongside. Body mass index, fat and lean mass, intracellular and extracellular water, and metabolic rate.

03

Cardio-wave analyser

Infrared at the fingernail. Rate and volume of blood flow, the elasticity of the main arteries, the smaller arteries and the capillaries, and heart frequencies.

04

Heart rate variability

The balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system, which together regulate heart rate, blood pressure, digestion and temperature.

05

Surface temperature

Taken at several sites, on both the left and the right, so that symmetry between the two sides can be compared rather than each reading taken alone.

06

Blood pressure, four quadrants

Both arms and both ankles, taken seated and again standing. Four quadrants rather than one arm, and two postures rather than one.

07

Spirometry

A full forced exhalation, giving forced vital capacity and forced expiratory volume in one second.

08

Blood oxygen

Pulse oximetry at the finger. The proportion of oxygen carried in the blood, where the usual range is 95 to 100 per cent.

09

Blood glucose

A capillary finger-prick sample, read on a standard meter at the point of care.

What we ask you to do beforehand

Give the urine and saliva samples first thing, before eating, drinking or cleaning your teeth. Beyond that, come as you normally are. Several of these readings move with a heavy meal, with alcohol and with caffeine in the preceding day, so we record what you have eaten and drunk rather than pretending it makes no difference. A reading taken the morning after a takeaway and a bottle of wine is a real reading of a real morning, and it is only misleading if nobody wrote down the morning.

The output

Forty minutes produces a decision, not a spreadsheet.

Every assessment resolves to one of three states, per person, on the front page. Occupational health does not need forty numbers per employee. It needs to know who is fine, who to watch, and who to send somewhere.

Within range

Every reading sits inside the expected range for age and sex. Recorded and baselined, so next year’s assessment has something to be compared against.

Worth keeping an eye on

Readings drifting towards the edge of the expected range. This does not mean anything is wrong. It means the number is worth understanding and worth measuring again.

Outside the expected range

One or more readings fall outside the range we would expect. We do not tell you what that means. We give you the number, the range, the date, and a clear recommendation to show the report to your GP.

Two kinds of finding, kept deliberately separate.

Conflating “worth understanding” with “take this to a doctor” is how health reports either frighten people or get ignored. These are split on the first page.

Evidence

We would rather tell you exactly what the research shows.

Most of this sector quotes a single flattering percentage and hopes nobody asks. The method behind HeartsSight was evaluated in controlled, blinded, ethically approved studies, and we would rather publish what those studies actually found, including where they found nothing.

Every figure below is traceable to a primary document, and the ethics approval exists in certified translation, so it can be checked rather than taken on trust.

What the studies support, and what they do not.

Well supported

  • Reproducibility. 47 people reassessed by different operators, four to sixty-four days apart, produced consistent readings.
  • Cardiovascular and body composition measurement. The most stable and consistent measurements across both research cohorts.
  • The relationship between measured physiology and how stressed people report feeling. Eleven measured factors accounted for 72% of variance in perceived stress on a validated instrument.
  • Delivery by trained non-clinicians. The method has been run twice inside other organisations by their own staff.
  • Completing the full measurement set inside forty minutes, repeatably, outside a clinic.

Not supported by our own data

  • Anything to do with the lungs, using the original instrument set. It showed no useful signal, and spirometry was added specifically in response.
  • Anything to do with blood sugar, using the original instrument set. A glucose meter was added specifically in response.
  • Anything structural. Nothing in the assessment touches the spine, the joints or the gut lining, and no reading should be read as if it did.
  • Any substitute for seeing a doctor. This tells you what your numbers are. It does not tell you what is wrong with you, and it is not intended to.
  • Any claim that a reading inside the expected range means you are well. It means that reading, on that day, was unremarkable.
The two instruments named above were added because our own research exposed those weaknesses. We think a development record is worth more than a headline number, and we would rather be the company that publishes both.

The original science

Stress is not a soft measure. We measure it.

The most distinctive finding in eighteen years of work is also the one nobody else is looking at. Independent statistical analysis found that eleven measured physiological factors accounted for 72% of the variance in participants' perceived stress scores, on the validated Perceived Stress Scale, with significant relationships to cardiac ejection time, very low frequency power, waist and hip dimensions, heart rate and blood pressure.

Not a questionnaire. Not a wellbeing score. The measurable physiology of allostatic load, taken in the same forty minutes as everything else, in a working population of firefighters, lifeguards and nurses.

That section of the independent report was added between drafts. The reviewers arrived at it from the data rather than being handed it as a premise, which is the strongest form a finding can take.

The hard questions

The things a serious buyer asks in the first ten minutes.

Answered here rather than in a meeting, because a buyer who has to extract these one at a time will assume the answers are worse than they are.

Is this a medical device, and is it regulated?

No, and we do not present it as one. HeartsSight is a health and wellbeing measurement service. It holds no UKCA or CE medical device certification, it is not offered for the diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of any disease, and nothing in your report will tell you that you have a condition.

Every instrument used on the day is an established, commercially available device, operated within its own manufacturer’s intended purpose. What we do is take those readings, present them against published reference ranges, and hand them to you and, if you choose, to your GP.

We are pursuing formal medical device certification for the analysis, because we would rather be able to say more than we can say today. Until that is in hand, the language on this page is deliberately limited, and we would rather lose a sale than borrow a status we have not earned.

Why does the assessment not require a clinician on site?

Because taking a standardised physiological measurement is not a clinical act. The operator is trained and certified to take readings correctly and to hand over a report. The report presents each reading against a published reference range. It does not interpret, and no clinical decision is made in the room.

Any decision about what a reading means belongs to the person’s own doctor, who has their history and can examine them. That is not a limitation we are apologising for. It is the correct place for that judgement to sit.

This is not theoretical. The method has been delivered twice inside other people's health systems by their own staff: three certified staff at a Russian federal medical centre, and two senior nurses at a Jerusalem teaching hospital after two weeks of training.

What happens if a reading is outside the expected range?

It is flagged clearly on the front page, shown with the expected range beside it, and the report recommends in plain words that the person takes it to their GP. The GP summary gives that clinician the same numbers in a form they can read quickly.

We do not name a condition, we do not name a test to request, and we do not decide urgency. If a reading is very far outside range, the operator is trained to say so calmly, to advise the person to contact their GP promptly, and to follow a written escalation procedure that includes calling 999 for the small number of readings where that is the right answer.

The assessment points people at their own doctor. It does not conclude for them, and it is not designed to.

You publish your weak results. Why?

Because they are findable. The studies are documented, the reports exist, and anyone conducting proper diligence will locate them. A company that has published its own null results has nothing left to be discovered.

It is also more useful. Knowing that the original instrument set produced nothing useful about the lungs, and that spirometry was added specifically in response, tells you considerably more about how this has been developed than a single headline percentage ever could.

What about the higher percentages published previously?

Earlier material quoted figures of 96.5% and 96.6%. Both are real numbers, and both describe something narrower than the phrasing suggested. The first is cumulative agreement reached across two years, including cases reclassified after later confirmation. The second is agreement against a composite clinical burden score rather than against angiography alone, where the figure is 77.4%.

We have reviewed every published claim against its source document and restated the ones that did not survive. The figures on this page are the ones that do.
